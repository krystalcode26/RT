### A Day in the Life

My alarm goes off at 8, and by 9:30 I'm in Daily Standup with coffee in hand. Anand(SM) runs it, fifteen minutes, video on. I say what I did yesterday — finished the `PUT /employees/{id}` update endpoint with optimistic locking via `@Version` so two HR admins can't overwrite each other's edits — what I'm doing today, which is starting the Department Assignment feature, and whether I'm blocked. 

Sandeep(DBA) mentions he's waiting on a decision about whether soft-deleted employees should still appear in historical department reports. Anand doesn't try to solve it in standup, he just says "let's take that offline" and loops in Angela right after.

After standup, Angela(PO), Vara(BA), Sandeep, and I hop on a quick call. Vara pulls up the original requirement doc and clarifies the business rule with Angela: soft-deleted employees stay visible in historical reports but disappear from active rosters. 

Sandeep and I align on the query filter in about ten minutes. That's basically how most "blockers" get resolved here which is a short, focused conversation right after standup, not a ticket sitting untouched for two days.

Mid-morning I'm heads-down in IntelliJ on the Employee Assignment endpoint, which calls into the Department service. I hit a weird 404, spend twenty minutes assuming my code's broken, then realize it's a path mismatch on the `@RequestMapping` in the Department controller. I fix it, write a unit test, and open a PR. Sarah(TL) reviews it within the hour, she reviews most of the backend PRs, and she flags one edge case I hadn't considered around reassigning employees across departments.

Around 1 PM, Eamonn(frontend) messages the team channel that the employee search UI needs the department *name* in the API response, not just the ID, so the dropdown doesn't show a raw UUID. I add the field to the DTO and ping him back within twenty minutes. Small, no-meeting-needed cross-functional exchange.

Early afternoon, Martin holds a quick architecture sync. Nothing urgent, just a standing thirty-minute session he runs weekly where he walks through a proposed change to how the Reporting service will consume events from the Employee and Department services, so we don't end up with tightly coupled point-to-point calls. Sarah, Sandeep, and I attend since it affects our services directly.

Later, Tara(DevOp) posts that CI is failing on the Docker build step after a base image version bump. She owns infra, so she takes it, but it blocks everyone from merging for about forty-five minutes until she patches the Dockerfile. Anand notes it for tomorrow's standup so we remember to raise pipeline stability in retro.

Late afternoon, Angie(QA) finds a bug in my department assignment logic through her automated regression suite and reassigning an employee doesn't remove them from their old department's roster, so they show up in two places at once. She logs it in Jira with the failing test case attached and tags me. It's small and I still have the context fresh, so I fix it before end of day.

I close out by updating my ticket, leaving a comment on a teammate's PR, and glancing at tomorrow's backlog — a Reporting story Vara hasn't fully fleshed out the acceptance criteria for yet, so I leave a question for her to clarify before I pick it up. A pretty normal day: standup, a couple of fast syncs, focused coding, a cross-team handoff, an architecture check-in, an infra hiccup, and a QA catch.

Martin, our Solution Architect, is less hands-on-keyboard day to day and more **focused on how all our services and domains fit** together.

**7. Who handles deployments and infrastructure on your team?**
Tara. Docker images, our **CI/CD pipeline, EC2 deployment, monitoring** that's her work. If the pipeline breaks, she's the first person we look to, though anyone can jump in on something simple like a Dockerfile typo.

### How Your Team Works

**1. How long are your sprints and why did you choose that length?**
Two weeks. Long enough to land a meaningful chunk of a feature Ex: a full CRUD flow or an auth feature  but short enough that Angela and stakeholders get regular checkpoints instead of long stretches of silence.

**2. Walk me through what happens during Sprint Planning.**
Angela(PO) brings a prioritized, mostly-**refined backlog**, with Vara(BA)'s acceptance criteria already attached. 

We go **story by story** — Angela gives business context, Martin flags anything with architectural weight, and the team asks clarifying questions and estimates using story points. 

Once we're aligned, we pull in stories until we hit our historical capacity, and we leave with a sprint goal and a committed sprint backlog.

**3. What does your Daily Standup look like — format, duration, who runs it?**
Fifteen minutes, every morning, Anand runs it. Each of us covers what we did yesterday, what we're doing today, and whether we're blocked. 

Anything that needs a longer conversation gets **pulled out of standup and scheduled separately**.

**4. What is a Sprint Review and who attends yours?**
End-of-sprint demo of working software, not slides. Lenny(PM), Angela, Anand(SM), the full engineering team, and usually a couple of HR stakeholders who'll actually use the product attend. Feedback from that session often reshapes what goes into the next sprint's backlog.

**5. What is a Retrospective and what does your team actually do in it?**
Right after review, **team-only,** no stakeholders. We talk about what went well, what didn't, and what to try differently, using a simple "**start, stop, continue**" format. 

Like after our CI pipeline broke mid-sprint — in retro we decided to start **pinning Docker base image versions instead of always pulling latest**.

**6. What is Backlog Refinement and when does your team do it?**
It's where Vara and Angela, with input from the team, **make sure upcoming stories have clear acceptance criteria, no open questions, and a rough estimate** before they hit planning. We do it once a week, mid-sprint, for about an hour, so Sprint Planning doesn't turn into a scramble to figure out what a story even means.

**7. What is your team's Definition of Done?**
Code is written, unit tested, and peer reviewed by Sarah or another engineer; it passes CI; 

it's **deployed to staging and verified** by Angie; acceptance criteria from Vara's requirements are met; 

and if it touches the database, Sandeep has reviewed any schema changes. 

Miss any of that, and it's not done — it just looks done.


### Communication & Escalation

**1. You found a critical bug two days before release — what do you do and who do you tell?**
I **flag it immediately in the team channel** and tag Anand(SM) and Angela(PO) directly rather than waiting for standup. 

Anand helps triage severity, and Angela makes the call on whether it's a release blocker. If it is, we drop lower-priority work and swarm on it until it's fixed and verified by Angie.

**2. A stakeholder wants to add a feature mid-sprint — how does your team handle it?**
It goes to Angela, not straight to engineering — often via Lenny or Vara first if it's a bigger strategic ask. 

Angela evaluates it against our current commitment. If it's truly urgent, she negotiates trading it for something else already in the sprint rather than piling it on top.

**3. You are blocked on a ticket and cannot finish before the sprint ends — who do you talk to?**
First Anand(SM), since unblocking people is his job. 

If it's technical, I'll also loop in Sarah or Martin depending on whether it's an implementation question or an architecture one. 

If it genuinely can't be resolved in time, it rolls into the next sprint and we talk about why in retro.

**4. Two engineers disagree on a technical approach — how is that resolved on your team?**
Usually it gets talked through directly, and if it's architecturally significant, Martin weighs in with the system-level view while Sarah weighs in on implementation tradeoffs. 

If we still can't agree, we **timebox a quick spike to test both approaches** rather than debating indefinitely.

**5. The designer is out sick and you need assets to move forward — what do you do?**
Because Gurudutt works a sprint ahead, this rarely blocks us directly. 

If it did, I'd use whatever's already approved in our design system and **flag the gap to Angela** so we don't guess at something that needs his eye later.

**6. Your deployment pipeline breaks and blocks the whole team — who owns that problem?**
Tara's. Everyone reprioritizes around getting it fixed since it blocks all our merges, but she leads the actual fix and decides the solution.


### Documentation & Artifacts

**1. What is a Product Backlog and who owns it on your team?**
It's the full ordered list of everything we might build — features, bugs, tech debt. 

Angela owns it and is the **only one who reprioritizes it,** though Lenny **shapes its direction** and Vara **feeds detailed requirements** into it, and anyone on the team can propose additions.

**2. What is a Sprint Backlog and how does it get created?**
The **subset of the product backlog** we commit to for the current sprint. We create it together in **Sprint Planning** by pulling refined, **estimated stories** off the top of the product backlog until we hit our capacity.

**3. When does your team write a technical design doc and who has to approve it?**
For anything touching multiple services or changing our data model significantly — like the Department Assignment feature — we write a short design doc. 

Martin usually leads it or reviews it for architectural soundness, Sarah signs off on the implementation approach, and Sandeep(DBA) reviews anything involving schema changes.

**4. How does your team file and track bugs?**
Everything goes into Jira on the same board as our feature work, tagged with a **severity level**. Angie files most of what her automated suites catch; anyone can file one they hit manually. **Critical bugs** get **pulled into the current sprint**; **lower severity ones go into the backlog** for Angela to prioritize.

**5. Where does your team write things down — what tools do you use and why?**
**Jira** for backlog, sprints, and tickets. 

**Confluence** for design docs, requirements from Vara, and meeting notes. 

**Slack** for day-to-day communication. 

**GitHub** for code and PR reviews. 

Keeping it to these four means there's always one obvious place to look instead of information scattered everywhere.


### Bigger Picture

**1. How does your team decide what goes into the next sprint?**
Lenny sets direction, Angela **prioritizes based on business value** and stakeholder needs with input from Vara's requirements work, but it's a conversation — during planning we push back if something's technically risky or dependencies aren't ready, and she adjusts. 

It's ultimately her call, but never made in a vacuum.

**2. What happens if your team consistently doesn't finish sprint work?**
We treat it as a **signal**, not something to punish. In retro we dig into why — overcommitting, stories not refined enough, too many interruptions — and **adjust our capacity planning or process** accordingly.

**3. How does a brand new feature go from idea to production on your team?**
It starts as an **idea from Lenny(PM)** or stakeholders, Vara(BA) turns it into **detailed requirements**, Angela(PO) **writes and prioritizes the story**, Martin **weighs in if it has architectural** impact, Gurudutt **designs it if it's user-facing**, it gets **picked up in a sprint**, **built and reviewed by engineering**, tested by Angie, and deployed by Tara through CI/CD.

Staging first, then production after verification.

**4. If someone asked you in an interview "describe your team" — what would you say in 60 seconds?**
I'd say I'm on a thirteen-person team building an internal Employee Management System for HR teams — employee records, department management, and reporting. 

We run two-week sprints, and we split product work between a Product Manager who owns vision and a Product Owner who owns the day-to-day backlog, with a Business Analyst nailing down requirements between them. 

On the engineering side, we've got dedicated architecture, technical leadership, backend, frontend, DevOps, database, and automated QA ownership rather than everyone wearing every hat. 

What I like about it is that specialization doesn't mean silos — escalation is fast and informal, and if something's blocking me, I'm usually unblocked within the hour instead of sitting in a queue for days.
